# Literature Search

Use when answering a question that depends on what is actually known — a scientific or
technical claim, a "what does the research say" request, a review of prior work.

## Frame the question

Convert the topic into a searchable question with named parts. For effects and interventions,
PICO works well: Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome. For other questions, name the
phenomenon, the context, and the outcome of interest.

"Does remote work affect productivity?" is a topic. "For software teams (P), does fully remote
work (I), compared with hybrid (C), change delivery throughput or defect rate (O)?" is a question.

## Search several sources, with a recorded strategy

- Combine synonyms with OR, concepts with AND; adapt the syntax per source.
- Use at least two kinds of source: an index (Google Scholar, PubMed, arXiv, Semantic Scholar) and citation chaining — look at what the included papers cite, and what cites them.
- Search for the **negative** result too ("no effect", "failed to replicate"); a search that can only find support is not a search.
- Record the exact queries, the source, and the date. A result set you cannot reproduce is an anecdote with a bibliography.

## Screen in two passes

1. **Title and abstract** against explicit inclusion criteria written before searching. Fix the criteria first so you cannot retro-fit them to the studies you happen to find.
2. **Full text** for the survivors. Record why each excluded item was excluded.

Prefer sources in this order when they conflict: systematic reviews and meta-analyses >
randomised or controlled studies > observational studies > expert opinion > press release >
the marketing page of the vendor selling the thing.

## Read a study for its weaknesses

For each retained source, ask:

- **Design** — is it capable of supporting the claim? A survey cannot establish causation.
- **Sample** — size, selection, who is missing. A study of undergraduates does not generalise to professionals.
- **Effect size**, not only significance. A statistically significant 0.5% improvement can be real and irrelevant.
- **Conflicts** — who funded it, who benefits from the result.
- **Replication** — has anyone independent found the same thing?
- **Recency** — has the field moved? Check the publication date against the current state.

## Synthesise honestly

- **Weigh by quality, not by count.** Three weak studies do not outweigh one strong one.
- **Report disagreement.** If the literature conflicts, say so and describe along what dimension (population, method, dose). A false consensus is worse than an acknowledged gap.
- **Separate what is established from what is hypothesised from what is unknown.** Three labeled sections, not one blended narrative.
- **Quantify where possible**: effect sizes with their intervals, sample sizes, dates.

## Citing

- Cite the specific claim's source, not the general topic's source. A citation that supports the paragraph's theme but not its sentence is a misattribution.
- Give enough to find the source: authors, year, title, venue or DOI.
- Never cite a source you did not open. Citing a paper because a blog post summarised it is how errors propagate.
- Quote exactly when the wording matters; paraphrase otherwise, and keep the meaning.

## Limits to state explicitly

- If a database is unreachable in your environment, say which, and that the search was partial.
- If you could only read abstracts, say so: abstracts exaggerate.
- If the question is outside the covered literature (very recent events, proprietary data), say that the answer is not research-backed.
