# Learning Explanation

Use when teaching a concept, writing an explainer, onboarding someone, or answering a
question that reveals a missing piece of understanding.

## Find the reader's actual level

A wrong calibration ruins the explanation more often than a wrong fact.

- Ask what they already know, or infer from their question — the vocabulary they use is the strongest signal.
- Explain from the nearest thing they already understand, not from first principles. An analogy to something they know beats a correct but abstract definition.
- Do not hedge by explaining three levels at once. Pick the level, give it, then offer the next one.

## Structure: concrete → abstract → concrete

1. **Start with a case they recognise.** A specific situation, the problem it creates, and what the concept does about it.
2. **Name the concept and state the rule** as compactly as possible.
3. **Return to a case**, one they have not seen, and walk through applying the rule.

An explanation that never leaves the abstract is a dictionary entry. One that never arrives at
the general rule is an anecdote.

## Techniques that work

- **Worked example before generalisation**: show one complete run, then state the pattern.
- **Contrast pairs**: explain what the concept is by showing what happens without it. "A lock is what stops two workers writing the same row" lands because you can see the collision.
- **Deliberate error**: show a plausible wrong answer and why it fails. It pre-empts the mistake the learner will otherwise make.
- **Progressive zoom**: the one-sentence version, then the paragraph, then the detail — and let the reader stop where they need to.
- **Checkpoint questions**: one question whose answer proves the previous section was understood.

## What to avoid

| Anti-pattern | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| Definition first, example later | The definition has no anchor to attach to |
| "Simply", "just", "obviously" | Tells the learner their difficulty is their fault |
| Complete taxonomy of every case | Overwhelms; the learner needs the common case first |
| Analogy stretched past its mapping | Creates a misconception that must be unlearned |
| Hiding the prerequisite | The learner fails at step 3 and blames themselves |
| No worked example | The rule stays verbal and unusable |

Name the limits of an analogy explicitly: "this holds for X; it breaks for Y because…".

## Explaining an error or a misunderstanding

1. Identify the specific belief that is wrong — not "you are confused", but "you are treating the index as the row".
2. Show why the belief is reasonable: it usually is, under a plausible assumption.
3. Give the counter-case that breaks it.
4. Restate the correct model in one sentence.

Correcting a wrong model with the right one, without addressing the wrong one, rarely works:
the wrong model survives underneath.

## Assessing understanding

Ask a question that cannot be answered by repeating the explanation: a transfer question (a new
case), a prediction question ("what happens if…"), or a reverse question ("what would you need
to see to conclude X"). A learner who can answer the nominal case only has memorised it.

## Limits

An explanation is not a substitute for practice. If the skill has a procedure, end with one
thing to try, not with a summary. And if the material depends on a version or a platform,
state the version you are explaining.
