# Editing And Revision

Use to improve an existing text — a draft, an email, a report, a translated text — without
losing the author's voice or the facts.

## Separate the passes

Editing everything at once produces a text that is locally correct and structurally wrong.
Do the passes in this order, and do not reword while restructuring.

1. **Intent** — does the text do what it is for? Who reads it, and what must they do after?
2. **Structure** — is the order of ideas the order the reader needs? Move whole paragraphs before touching sentences.
3. **Content** — is every claim true, supported, and necessary? Cut what does not earn its place.
4. **Sentences** — length, ambiguity, connectives.
5. **Surface** — grammar, spelling, punctuation, typography.

## The editing checklist that finds the most

**Redundancy**

- The same idea twice in different words (very common in LLM drafts and in translation).
- A sentence that restates the heading.
- Introductions that announce what will be said, followed by saying it.

**Vagueness**

- "Significant", "several", "improved", "efficient" — replace with the number or the fact, or delete.
- "Etc." — the reader needs the rule, not a trailing hint.
- Passive voice where the actor matters ("mistakes were made").

**Density**

- Nominalisations: "the implementation of the optimisation" → "optimising".
- Stacked prepositional phrases: "a review of the impact of the change on the team" → "how the change affects the team".
- "There is/are" openings: usually a weak verb hiding a strong one.

**Rhythm**

- Three consecutive sentences of the same length read as machine output. Vary.
- One-sentence paragraphs are a valid tool, not an error.

## Preserving voice

- Keep the author's distinctive words unless they are wrong. Correcting "utilise" to "use" is fine; flattening every metaphor into neutrality is a rewrite.
- Match register to the audience, not to your preference: a legal notice and a launch post should not read alike.
- When the source is a translation, preserve the source's intent, not its syntax. A literal translation of a French subordinate clause is not better English.

## Cutting — the arithmetic of editing

Most drafts are 20–40% too long, and the fat is concentrated in the same places: openings,
transitions, hedges, restatements, and closing summaries.

Delete in this order, re-reading after each cut:

1. The first paragraph, if it only announces the topic.
2. Every adverb modifying an already-strong verb ("completely eliminate").
3. Every sentence beginning "It is important to note that".
4. The final paragraph, if it only repeats the document.
5. Any example that illustrates the previous example.

## What never to change without asking

- Facts, figures, names, dates, citations, legal or medical wording.
- Quoted material, including its errors — mark `[sic]` instead of silently fixing.
- The author's stated position, even if you disagree.
- Formatting conventions the author uses consistently (spelling variant, capitalisation style).

## Delivering the revision

Show what changed and why:

- For a short text: the revised text, then a short list of substantive changes.
- For a long text: a change log by section, with the categories above, and the revised file.
- Flag anything you could not verify (a statistic, a quotation) as an open question rather than smoothing it over.

Never present a rewrite as a proofread. If you restructured the argument, say so.
